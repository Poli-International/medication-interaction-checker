🛡️ Studio Safety & Clinical Reference

Essential clinical reference for clients, tattoo artists, and piercers on managing common procedural challenges associated with medications.

1. Painkillers & Pre-Procedure Pain Management

Clients often take over-the-counter painkillers before a session to manage pain. It is vital to understand the difference between drug classes:

  • Aspirin & NSAIDs (Ibuprofen, Naproxen, Nurofen, Aleve): Inhibit platelet aggregation and increase bleeding and plasma oozing during tattooing. Oozing pushes ink out of the skin, causes excessive scab formation, and delays healing. Avoid 24–48 hours before sessions unless prescribed.
  • Paracetamol / Acetaminophen (Tylenol, Panadol): Does NOT affect platelet function or bleeding. It is the preferred mild analgesic before or after procedures if approved by a doctor.

2. Topical Numbing Creams & Local Anaesthetics

Numbing creams (Lidocaine, Tetracaine, Prilocaine, TKTX) have exploded in popularity, but introduce specific risks:

  • Skin Texture Alterations: High-potency creams cause localized edema (swelling) and toughening, making skin spongy or rubbery, which hinders needle penetration and line precision.
  • Rebound Pain & Vasoconstriction: Creams containing Epinephrine temporarily constrict blood vessels. As the epinephrine wears off, severe rebound hyperemia (flushing and rapid bleeding) can occur.
  • Toxicity & Methemoglobinemia Risk: Covering large body areas in thick lidocaine under plastic wrap can cause systemic absorption, leading to toxicity, rapid heart rate, or blood oxygenation drops.

3. Fainting & Vasovagal Syncope Risk Factors

Vasovagal syncope is a sudden drop in heart rate and blood pressure triggered by pain, nervous tension, or long sessions:

  • High Risk Medications: Beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists (Ozempic), ADHD stimulants, and anti-anxiety meds increase fainting risk.
  • Prevention Protocol: Eat a complex carbohydrate meal 1–2 hours before the session. Keep sugary drinks (fruit juice, soda) at the station. If feeling lightheaded, lay flat with legs elevated immediately.

4. Adhesive Aftercare Bandages (Saniderm / Tegaderm) & Skin Thinning

  • Patients on topical retinoids, systemic steroids, or with thin skin are at high risk of skin tearing, blisters, or contact dermatitis when removing second-skin bandages.
  • In these cases, traditional breathable cling wrap or non-stick pads with gentle hypoallergenic tape are recommended.

5. Bloodborne Pathogens & Cross-Contamination Containment

  • Barrier Protection: All studio touch surfaces (clip cords, spray bottles, power supplies, armrests) must be wrapped in disposable medical-grade barrier sleeves or film before every client.
  • Single-Use Cartridges & Needles: Verify that sterile needle blister packs are opened directly in front of the client. Ensure backflow membrane protection on cartridge needles.
  • Sharps Container Disposal: All used needles and scalpels must be disposed of immediately into marked rigid biohazard sharps containers.

6. Alcohol, Cannabis & Recreational Substance Intake Policy

  • Informed Consent & Legal Safety: Studios strictly cannot legally obtain informed consent from clients under acute intoxication from alcohol, cannabis, or illicit drugs.
  • Physiological Compromise: Alcohol causes heavy plasma weeping; cannabis causes vasodilation and panic sensitivity; stimulants cause muscle tremors and heart strain. Intoxicated clients must be rescheduled.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

NEVER stop or adjust prescribed medication without explicit authorization from your prescribing doctor. Stopping blood thinners, cardiac medication, or psychiatric drugs can pose serious health risks far outweighing procedure benefits. If a medication is a contraindication (e.g. Accutane), delay the procedure until after completing your course and waiting the recommended washout period.

Isotretinoin fundamentally alters skin cell turnover, thins the stratum corneum, and suppresses sebum glands. Tattooing or piercing during or shortly after Accutane treatment leads to unpredictable ink fallout, severe scarring, hypertrophic tissue, and non-healing piercing fistulas. Most professionals require waiting 6 to 12 months after the last dose.

It is strongly discouraged. THC causes vasodilation (blood vessel opening), raises baseline heart rate, and can significantly heighten anxiety, paranoia, or pain sensitivity during skin puncture. Many professional artists will refuse to tattoo clients under acute influence.

Alcohol thins your blood and acts as a diuretic, leaving you severely dehydrated. During tattooing, thin blood causes heavy plasma weeping that pushes ink pigment out of dermal layers and causes thick, patchy scabs. A hangover also dramatically lowers your pain threshold and blood sugar, making fainting far more likely.

Nicotine is a powerful microvascular vasoconstrictor. It shrinks tiny capillaries near the surface of your skin, restricting oxygenated blood supply needed for dermal cell repair and piercing fistula formation. Heavy nicotine use leads to delayed healing, higher infection risk, and increased piercing rejection rates.

Limit high caffeine intake right before your session. Caffeine raises resting pulse rate and blood pressure, increases muscle jitteriness, and can heighten nervous tension. A small cup of coffee is fine, but avoid large energy drinks or pre-workout supplements prior to tattooing.

Bring a complete list of your medications (or use our Artist Consultation Brief feature), inform your artist/piercer during intake, bring snacks/sugary drinks if you take diabetes or blood pressure medications, and bring a physician's clearance note if taking blood thinners or immunosuppressants.

It is strongly recommended to avoid NSAIDs (Ibuprofen, Naproxen) and Aspirin 24–48 hours before tattooing or piercing because they thin blood and increase procedural bleeding. If you need mild pain relief, Acetaminophen (Paracetamol) is generally safer as it does not affect clotting.

Avoid heavy exercise for 24–48 hours to prevent excessive sweating, stretching, or friction on fresh wounds. Swimming in pools, lakes, rivers, or ocean water is strictly prohibited for 2–4 weeks until completely healed, as standing water contains bacterial pathogens that easily cause severe skin infections.

💫

Vasovagal Syncope & Fainting Protocol

Common response to sudden pain, anxiety, or low blood sugar causing temporary cerebral hypoperfusion.

  1. Stop Procedure Immediately: Pull needle away, secure equipment, and ensure client cannot fall from table/chair.
  2. Lay Flat & Elevate Legs: Place client completely flat on their back and elevate legs 30° above heart level (Trendelenburg position) to quickly restore blood flow to the brain.
  3. Cool & Ventilate: Place a cold, damp paper towel on their forehead or nape of neck. Ensure good air circulation. Loosen tight collars/clothing.
  4. Glucose Boost: Once fully conscious and alert, offer cold fruit juice, soda, or glucose tablets. Keep them lying down for at least 15–20 minutes before attempting to sit up slowly.
🐝

Acute Allergic Reaction & Anaphylaxis

Allergic response to topical numbing creams, latex gloves, stencil chemicals, or inks.

  1. CALL EMERGENCY SERVICES NOW: Throat tightness, noisy or strained breathing, swelling of the tongue or lips, or severe breathing difficulty means anaphylaxis. Call immediately, before anything else, and say the word "anaphylaxis". Do not wait to see whether it settles.
  2. Remove the Trigger: Stop the procedure, wipe away all topical creams and soaps with sterile saline, and switch to non-latex gloves.
  3. Keep Them Lying Flat: Lay the client flat with legs raised, unless breathing is easier sitting up. Do not stand them up or walk them anywhere, even if they say they feel better.
  4. Their Own Auto-Injector: If the client carries their own prescribed adrenaline auto-injector (EpiPen or similar), help them reach it and use it themselves, into the outer mid-thigh. It is their device and their decision. Never use a device prescribed to someone else, and never keep or administer one of your own.
  5. Stay With Them: Symptoms can return after they improve. Stay beside the client until the ambulance arrives, and hand over the auto-injector so responders can see what was given and when.
🩸

Excessive Bleeding & Heavy Plasma Oozing

Triggered by recent NSAIDs, blood thinners, alcohol intake, or high blood pressure.

  1. Direct Firm Pressure: Place sterile dry gauze over the bleeding site and hold firm, continuous direct pressure for 5–10 full minutes without lifting.
  2. Elevate Area: Position the bleeding limb or area elevated above the level of the heart.
  3. Pressure Wrap: Apply a clean sterile pressure dressing once bleeding slows down.
  4. Bright, Spurting Blood: Call Immediately. Blood that pulses or spurts in time with the heartbeat is arterial. Call emergency services straight away, keep firm direct pressure on the site, and do not wait out any timer. Separately, call if heavy bleeding is still going after 15 minutes of continuous firm pressure.
🫁

Hyperventilation & Acute Panic Attack

Rapid shallow breathing causing lightheadedness, tingling fingers, and muscle tightness.

  1. Pause & Reassure: Step back, remove needles/stencils from view, and speak in a calm, reassuring, grounded voice.
  2. 4-4-4 Box Breathing: Instruct client to breathe with you: Inhale deeply through nose for 4s, hold breath for 4s, slow exhale through mouth for 4s. Repeat 5 times.
  3. Physical Grounding: Have client place both feet flat on the floor and focus on feeling the solid ground beneath them. Provide a cold glass of water.
